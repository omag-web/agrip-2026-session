Viewing Your Website Through Your Members’ Eyes

The organizations in this room provide different services, but we all have one thing in common:

We exist for our members.

And those members come to our websites for different reasons.

  • Training.
  • Benefits.
  • Safety resources.
  • Claims information.
  • Grants.
  • Programs.
  • Advice.

But almost every visitor arrives with one thing in common:

They have a task.

They’re trying to answer questions like:

  • Where do I go?
  • What do I need?
  • Who can help me?
  • What do I do next?

The challenge is that we often organize websites around how our organizations work internally.

  • Departments.
  • Programs.
  • Teams.

That makes sense to us because we already understand the organization.

But the person visiting for the first time may have no idea which department owns the answer they need.

So before we evaluate a page, we need to identify two things:

Who is using this page?

And:

What are they trying to accomplish?

Pause

Think back to that employee who started Monday.

What would they call the thing they’re looking for?

And does your website use the same words?

Next · 05 Five Signs Your Website Needs Attention

Slide 05/ 23

Five Signs Your Website Needs Attention

There are a few common signs that members may be doing more work than they should have to do.

1 · Hard to find

First, people struggle to find things.

If staff repeatedly has to send people the same link, that may be telling you something.

2 · Buried

Second, important information is buried.

The answer is technically there, but somebody has to work to find it.

3 · Inconsistent

Third, different parts of the website work differently.

Someone learns how one section works, moves somewhere else, and has to start over.

4 · No next step

Fourth, there’s no obvious next step.

They understand the program, but they don’t know how to register, apply, download something, or contact somebody.

5 · Built for the org

And fifth, the website assumes people already understand the organization.

It expects visitors to know our departments, acronyms, terminology, and internal structure.

Wrap

Those are clues.

Instead of saying, “Our website needs work,” they give us something specific to investigate.

Next · 06 The FIND Framework

Slide 06/ 23

The FIND Framework

To make that review easier and more consistent, I use four questions.

Together, they form the FIND Framework.

F · Findable

Can someone locate what they need?

Do the menu labels, page titles, and links help them get there?

I · Intuitive

Once they arrive, does the experience make sense without somebody having to explain it?

Can they quickly understand what they’re looking at?

N · Necessary

Does the content on this page help the person complete the task they came here to do?

Something can be useful information and still be in the wrong place.

D · Directed

Does the page tell them what to do next?

  • Register.
  • Apply.
  • Download.
  • Contact someone.

Whatever the next step is, can they see it?

Pause

Those four questions give us a shared way to evaluate a page.

And they give AI something much more useful than: “Tell me what you think of my website.”

Next · 07 Using AI to Audit and Improve Your Website

Slide 07/ 23

Using AI to Audit and Improve Your Website

“Review my website” is a very broad request.

  • AI might comment on colors.
  • Rewrite a headline.
  • Tell you to simplify the navigation.

Some of that may be useful.

But the question I care about is:

Can the person using this page accomplish what they came here to do?

That’s where FIND helps.

AI can flag confusing wording.

It can help organize resources around member tasks.

It can suggest more consistent ways to structure similar pages.

Example

For example, imagine a training page with upcoming courses, registration information, old presentations, and recordings all mixed together.

All of that information may be useful.

But the person who came there to register for a class shouldn’t have to sort through everything else first.

Now we’ve given AI a real problem:

How do we make this page work better for that task?

Brief pause

Practical point

Make sure AI can actually see what you’re asking it to review.

Depending on the tool, that might mean giving it page text, screenshots, PDFs, files, or other material.

And ask it what it could actually access.

There’s a big difference between reviewing an entire webpage and reviewing text you pasted into a chat.

Next · 10 Turning the Framework Into an AI Audit

Slide 10/ 23

Turning the Framework Into an AI Audit

If I simply tell AI: “Review this page.”

I’m forcing it to make assumptions.

  • Who uses it?
  • What are they trying to accomplish?
  • What matters?
  • What does success look like?

We can give it a better assignment.

Gesture to slide

There are three things I want to give AI.

1 · The Lens

First, the lens.

We’ve already established ours: the member experience through FIND.

But we also need a specific task.

Instead of: “Review our training page.”

We might say:

Review this training page from the perspective of a new employee trying to register for their first course.

Now AI knows whose experience we care about.

2 · The Standards

Move to standards

Second, give it the standards.

  • Maybe you want plain language.
  • Descriptive links.
  • Consistent terminology.
  • A professional, approachable tone.

If you have brand or content guidelines, include them.

You can also ask it to flag potential accessibility concerns.

WCAG stands for Web Content Accessibility Guidelines.

AI may help identify issues, but remember that its assessment is limited by what it can actually see.

3 · The Background

Move to background

Third, give it the background.

Maybe registration happens in a separate system.

Maybe certain courses have eligibility requirements.

Maybe users need to know whether something is online or in person.

That context matters.

Without it, AI might suggest a solution that sounds good but doesn’t fit how your organization actually works.

Pause

Put it together

So instead of: “Review this page.”

We could say:

Review this training page for a new employee who wants to register for a course. Use the FIND Framework. Registration takes place in a separate system. Identify anything that makes it difficult to choose a course or understand how to register. Recommend specific improvements and flag anything staff needs to verify.

Now AI has:

  • A person.
  • A task.
  • A framework.
  • And context.

TransitionThe instructions shape the review. But the format of the answer matters too.

Next · 11 What a Useful AI Audit Looks Like

Slide 11/ 23

What a Useful AI Audit Looks Like

When AI gives me an audit, I want something I can make decisions from.

I want three things:

  • Issues found.
  • Recommended fixes.
  • And review needed.

Issues Found

I want the problem to be specific.

“The page is confusing” isn’t very useful.

But:

The registration link appears only at the bottom of the page and it isn’t clear which courses it applies to.

Now I have something I can investigate.

Recommended Fixes

Then give me a change I could actually make.

For example:

Move registration instructions closer to the course listings.

That’s actionable.

Ask audience

AskWhich is easier to hand somebody on your team? “Improve the user experience.” Or: “Add registration instructions beside the course listings.”

Pause

Specific recommendations move us from feedback to work.

Review Needed

And finally, what needs human review?

  • Who is eligible?
  • Is there a fee?
  • Is the deadline current?
  • Does somebody need an account?

AI can flag those questions.

The person responsible for the service needs to answer them.

Pause

So after an audit, I want to know:

  • What might be getting in the member’s way?
  • What could we change?
  • And what do we need to verify first?

Choosing the Right AI Workspace

You’re going to see a lot of different AI tools and workspaces.

My advice is simple:

Don’t start with the tool. Start with the task.

  • What are you trying to review?
  • And what does the AI need access to?

If you’re reviewing one webpage, like the one you picked earlier, sharing the content in chat may be enough.

If you’re working across multiple files or a larger website project, you may need a workspace that can access more material.

But you don’t have to start big.

One page is enough.

Next · 20 Turning FIND Into a Claude Skill

Turning FIND Into a Claude Skill

That’s where the FIND Skill comes in.

Think of a Skill as a reusable set of instructions.

Instead of explaining the framework from scratch every time, the Skill carries the process with it.

Gesture to slide

It can ask for the same kinds of outputs every time:

  • A summary.
  • A FIND scorecard.
  • Specific issues.
  • Recommended fixes.
  • And questions that require human review.

That gives us a consistent starting point across multiple pages.

Pause

Scorecard caution

One caution about the scorecard.

The number itself isn’t the important part.

If AI says your page is a six out of ten, please don’t go home and announce: “Our website got a six.”

Read the explanation. The evidence behind the score is what matters.

Pause

The prompt builder helps you create a prompt for one page.

The Skill helps you repeat a defined review process.

Both let you start small.
